# Core shadow and halftone

In the last lesson the ball split into light and shadow. But a real ball has no sharp border: light turns into shadow smoothly. That transition is what makes the form round.

## The light-and-shadow scale

On a lit ball, from light to shadow:

1. **Highlight** — the reflection of the source, the lightest point.
2. **Light** — the part facing the source.
3. **Halftone** — the light falls more and more obliquely, the tone gradually darkens.
4. **Terminator** — the border of light and shadow.
5. **Core shadow** — the side facing away from the light.
6. **Reflected light** — light bounced onto the edge in the shadow (lesson 4).
7. **Cast shadow** — the shadow the object throws on the floor (lesson 3).

Artists have learned this scale on a plaster ball for centuries: it is the same for an apple, a head and a fold of cloth.

## Where the darkest part is

Beginners paint the shadow equally dark all the way to the edge. In fact the darkest spot is **right past the terminator**. Further towards the edge, light bounced from the floor and the surroundings lightens the shadow a little.

## Halftones are longer than they seem

The halftone takes up most of the lit side. Make it narrow and the ball looks like a flat disc with a dark rim. Build the transition with a wide soft brush, in layers of 15–20%.

## Technique

- A soft brush: hardness 0–30%.
- Low opacity: the shadow is **built up**, not laid down in one stroke.
- Strokes follow the form: an arc along the terminator, not across it.
- Shadows are handy on a separate layer in “Multiply” mode — then they darken without changing the colour.

## Sensei's advice

A shadow is not black paint but an absence of light. Add it little by little and step back often: a shadow that looks weak up close is often just right from a distance.

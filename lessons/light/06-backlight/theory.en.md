# Backlight and atmosphere

Backlight (contre-jour) is light coming from behind an object, towards the viewer. A sunset behind a tree, a lamp behind the head of the person you are talking to, the sun through leaves. It is one of the most expressive and most difficult kinds of lighting.

## What we see

The source is behind the ball, so the ball faces us with its **shadow side**:

- the ball is an almost **solid dark silhouette**;
- along the edge, where the rays touch the form, there is a **thin glowing outline**;
- around it there is a **halo**: the light scatters in the air.

The form reads not through light and shadow but through the silhouette and the outline. So the silhouette must be expressive: in contre-jour nothing will save it.

## The glowing outline

The outline is brightest where the rays graze the form. If the source is slightly above the object, the top edge is strongest. The outline:

- is narrow: the further in from the edge, the faster it fades;
- is warm if the source is warm;
- lies **inside** the silhouette.

## Atmosphere

In perfectly clear air there is no halo. But air is never clear: dust, moisture and smoke scatter light, and a glow appears around the object. The thicker the haze, the wider and softer the halo.

This is part of **aerial perspective**: the air between us and the object becomes visible. In Japanese ink painting, mist and haze are the heroes of a landscape: mountains emerge from white emptiness, and the emptiness works as light.

## Technique

1. A new layer for the glow.
2. A huge soft brush, opacity 15–30%: the halo in several passes, brighter at the edge of the silhouette.
3. A thin brush, a light warm colour: the outline along the edge, brighter at the top.
4. The “Color Dodge” or “Screen” layer mode makes the light richer.
5. Leave the middle of the silhouette alone.

## Sensei's advice

The lightest spot in a picture looks light only next to something dark. Contre-jour is a lesson in contrast: a dark silhouette turns light into radiance.
